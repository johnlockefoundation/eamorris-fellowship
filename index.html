About the fellowship

A legacy of leadership, carried forward.

The E.A. Morris Fellowship for Emerging Leaders was established to honor the memory of Edwin A. Morris and accepted its inaugural class of Fellows in 2006.

Ed Morris passed away on July 31, 1998, but through the E.A. Morris Charitable Foundation and this Fellowship, his legacy of leadership continues — training and energizing new leaders for the future of North Carolina.

The program is administered by the John Locke Foundation. In each of its years of operation it has graduated outstanding Fellows in diverse professional fields, and this experience has already begun to generate a growing base of emerging leaders who understand how to bring people and institutions together.

In memory of

Edwin A. Morris, 1903–1998

Inducted into the North Carolina Business Hall of Fame, 1993

One of the country's premier marketing and clothing executives, Edwin A. Morris built the world's largest manufacturer of work and recreational clothing during the mid-twentieth century: Blue Bell, Inc.

Born in Cabarrus County on August 13, 1903, the youngest of five children, Morris grew up picking cotton on a farm in Concord, earning 50 cents for every 100 pounds he picked. He received a bachelor's degree in business administration from Washington and Lee University in 1926, attended Harvard Business School for a year, and then went to work for a Massachusetts textile firm.

It all started with overalls

In 1904, C.C. Hudson and Associates launched the Hudson Overalls Company in Greensboro. After buying out his partners, Hudson renamed the company Blue Bell. In 1937, Edwin Morris joined Blue Bell-Globe Manufacturing as plant manager in Abingdon, Illinois; four years later he became executive vice president in charge of manufacturing.

In 1948, Morris became president and CEO. A marketing whiz, he saw potential in a little-used brand the company had acquired: Wranglers. Eager to challenge Levi Strauss and Lee, Blue Bell enlisted world-champion cowboys to wear-test and endorse the jeans, and diversified into dungarees, shirts, women's and children's clothing, and much more.

By 1955 the firm had $53 million in sales, making it the world's largest producer of work and play clothes. Morris added four new plants in his first seven years as president, bringing the total to 28 plants in seven states. He was elected chairman of the board in 1966 and remained CEO until 1971, serving as chairman until his retirement in 1981.

Personally frugal and unassuming, Morris nonetheless counted among his friends and associates leaders and emerging leaders in business, politics, and academia — including Jesse Helms, Lauch Faircloth, Terry Sanford, Joseph Bryan, and Jim Martin.

In 1980 he was awarded an honorary doctor of law degree from Washington and Lee University, the same year the E.A. Morris Charitable Foundation was established. After a cancer diagnosis late in life, his initial $1 million gift in 1978 to the Duke Comprehensive Cancer Center was followed by others to improve the health and survival of patients seeking treatment there. The Foundation has also been a generous benefactor of the Greensboro Public Library, Washington and Lee University, the John Locke Foundation, and many religious and civic organizations.

Legacy becomes a fellowship

The Fellowship grew out of several organizational ideas focused on citizenship and free enterprise. One was the North Carolina Institute for Citizenship, conceived in early 2003 to support citizens actively involved in government. Out of it grew a related concept, the North Carolina Free Enterprise Institute, whose mission narrowed to focus on emerging leaders aged 25–35 and incorporated training in effective communication and other skills.

On August 13, 2003 — the centennial of Mr. Morris's birth — a discussion to renew that mission took place in Raleigh under the sponsorship of the E.A. Morris Foundation. The organization was incorporated as the Center for Citizenship, Enterprise, and Government under longtime community leader Jim Lofton.

Jim Lofton's death in 2006 left those plans unfulfilled. Rather than abandon the vision of training and promoting young leaders inspired by Ed Morris, the CCEG, E.A. Morris, and John Locke Foundation boards agreed to transfer responsibility for the leadership program to the John Locke Foundation — a natural fit, as the E.A. Morris Foundation had been a contributing founder of JLF in 1990, and the missions were closely aligned.

With thanks to Mr. John Thomas, President of the E.A. Morris Charitable Foundation, and the E.A. Morris Board, for their continued financial and moral support of this Fellowship.

The program

What the fellowship includes

Intensive seminars and leadership training, public policy events, directed readings, and the tools to lead on a local problem.

Leadership retreats

Three mandatory retreats held at different locations around the state each year, developing the individual Fellow's leadership skills and building the class into a network.

Local forums

Four events or conferences over the year, sponsored by universities, civic groups, think tanks, and other organizations — chosen by each Fellow from an approved list.

A leadership project

The focus of the yearlong effort: a volunteer, nonprofit, business-led, or public-sector undertaking in which Fellows develop a team and lead.

Public recognition

Fellows' commitment and hard work are formally recognized at the Hello/Farewell gala, closing out the Fellowship year.

The Fellowship develops further understanding of

  • Spotting opportunities for leadership in a local community
  • Ethics for developing leadership
  • Elements of fundraising and philanthropy
  • Program planning and evaluation
  • Economic analysis and how to use it
  • Speaking in public and in the media
  • Modern electoral politics
  • Emotional intelligence and organizational effectiveness
  • Finding and cultivating like-minded volunteers, donors, and activists
